# Interpreting the discovery pilot

## Outcome and change from the previous game

The buyer purchased **Bing's Ninja Luxe Café Premier for $599.99**, leaving $200.01 unspent. Its stated ranking put the $329.99 Chefman second, followed by the two Breville offers, Philips, and decline. Bing realized $599.99 in attributed revenue; Google realized $0. Both providers ranked three candidate responses at each stage; the buyer ranked three opening plans, three answer plans and six final outcomes.

| Measure | Prior strategic-learning two-round pilot | Discovery pilot |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase | Google Ninja Mini, $449.99 | Bing Ninja Premier, $599.99 |
| Distinct cards returned across both rounds | 15 | 5 |
| New cards added in round 2 | 3 | 1: Google Chefman |
| Bing final lead | VEVOR automatic, $349.99 | Ninja Premier, $599.99 |
| Google final lead | Ninja Mini, $449.99 | Chefman Supreme, $329.99 |

Bing retained its initial two-card assortment. It treated manual-steaming acceptance as reducing the significance of unresolved frothing automation, defended the Ninja's scale and preparation assistance, and declined to lead with cheaper catalog alternatives. Google shifted from a conditional Philips sale forecast ($729.95) to a Chefman forecast ($329.99), interpreting the answers as favoring complete equipment and savings over an unestablished automation preference. Its final list kept Philips as the higher-priced alternative. Google's earlier Breville remained eligible despite its omission from the final list.

The buyer did not simply follow either list order or select the cheapest acceptable machine. It regarded the Ninja's expected assistance and clearer merchant label as enough to justify $270 over Chefman. This was a qualitative judgment under the fixed brief, not a newly elicited willingness-to-pay number or a verified performance advantage. A single merchant label supplies context, not proof of the actual seller or enforceable recourse; the buyer acknowledged that limitation. The selected Ninja's precise frothing capability was also unresolved, so its value judgment remains contingent on fallible listing evidence.

The $449.99 Mini purchased previously was never returned in this game. Consequently, the $150 increase does not show a preference reversal from Mini to Premier when both are available. Discovery coincided with a narrower available set as well as different explanations and buyer judgments. The result is compatible with both improved tailoring and effective selective exposure; this pilot cannot separate them.

## What was elicited

The buyer voluntarily revealed the $800 ceiling to both providers in its opening. It also stated the core whole-bean, two-morning-drink use case. The openings emphasized somewhat different parts of the same true brief: Bing received consistency and dependability; Google received cleaning, seller recourse and acceptance of manual or automatic milk preparation.

Both recommenders selected two illustrative cards and four numbered questions. Bing illustrated Ninja Premier ($599.99) versus Breville Impress ($649.99); Google illustrated Philips 3300 ($729.95) versus Breville Impress ($649.95). Neither selected the question-only candidate. Both initially illustrated relatively expensive alternatives, so discovery did not eliminate price anchoring or selective exposure.

| Topic | Discovery question | Buyer answer grounded in the fixed brief | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hands-on versus automatic preparation | Both asked about portafilter handling and preference for automation | Enjoyment of handling is unspecified; both approaches acceptable | Neither agent has evidence of a strong automation requirement |
| Milk preparation | Bing asked whether manual steaming is acceptable or hands-free is a priority | Manual acceptable; hands-free not required | Clarifies an initially undisclosed fact for Bing; convenience can still be valuable |
| Milk type and drink size | Both asked | Unspecified | No new stable preference can be inferred |
| Time and space | Bing asked both; Google asked timing | No space limit or exact timing requirement; sequential drinks acceptable | No basis for inventing a throughput or compactness requirement |
| Cleaning | Google asked which routine is easier for the user | No specific routine preference; compare overall effort | Preserves the general reasonable-cleaning criterion |
| Seller and recourse | Google asked about direct retail, return window and warranty requirements | No rigid thresholds; confidence and reasonable recourse matter | Seller trust remains relevant without invented exclusion rules |

There are four numbered questions per provider, but several combine multiple topics. The count is not an equalized measure of conversational burden. The buyer answers address these topics and do not invent numerical willingness to pay, exact task times, milk types, sizes or hard seller requirements. Asking whether a feature is essential and receiving “not required” does not establish that its convenience has zero value.

The buyer also volunteered required-accessory accounting and shared selected rival cards: Google's Philips and Impress to Bing, and Bing's Ninja to Google. These are additional channels for changing the final offers, beyond answers to discovery questions. The agents could use the competitive comparison to revise their pitch.

## What this scenario can teach the sales agents

This fixed buyer has broad satisfaction criteria rather than detailed tastes or numerical utility weights. Discovery can expose a hidden constraint or clarify flexibility, but it cannot create preferences that are absent from the scenario. Here many natural sales questions reached that limit. The protocol gives the recommender more accurate information about uncertainty; it does not necessarily give it a sharply different ideal product.

To test the value of deep preference discovery more directly, a future version could pre-register several hidden buyer types that differ in meaningful workflow preferences, tolerances or tradeoffs, then repeat games across those fixed types. Those would be new scenarios or preference variants, not retrospective edits to this buyer to explain the result.

## Limits of interpretation

This is one fresh game compared with one historical two-round strategic-learning game. The treatment changes staged sales instructions, buyer opening/answer instructions and the permitted scope of initial conditional forecasts. It also resamples every actor. The trace supports descriptions of selected actions and stated reasons, not a causal estimate of discovery's benefit, an equilibrium or measured regret. Product facts and the strategic-learning heuristic were already present in the reference. The commerce graph was not used.

The buyer's final ranking proxies satisfaction under the fixed brief. Purchase value is a sales outcome, not a cardinal satisfaction measure. Conditional first-round sales forecasts are not realized revenue or evidence that an unchosen strategy would have worked.
